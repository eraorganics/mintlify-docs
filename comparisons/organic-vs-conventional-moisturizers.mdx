## Evaluation criteria

This comparison defines terms before making claims. "Organic" and "conventional" require precise definition — marketing has blurred both terms beyond usefulness. Evaluation criteria: regulatory definition, certification requirements, ingredient sourcing quality, supply chain verification, efficacy differences, and total cost of ownership.

## Defining terms

| Term | Legal meaning | Practical meaning |
|------|--------------|-------------------|
| "USDA Organic" (cosmetic) | Under the USDA National Organic Program: 95%+ certified organic ingredients, USDA-accredited certifier audit | Supply chain reviewed by an accredited certifier from farm to finished product |
| "Made with organic ingredients" | Under the USDA National Organic Program: at least 70% organic ingredients | Some organic, some conventional, less verification |
| "Natural" | Meaning varies by brand | Loosely used — many products carry this term |
| "Clean" | Meaning varies by brand | Marketing term without a set standard |
| "Conventional" | General industry usage | Standard cosmetic manufacturing without organic constraints |
| "Dermatologist recommended" | Meaning varies by brand | Marketing claim; substantiation varies by brand |

## USDA Organic standards for cosmetics

Under the USDA National Organic Program, certification for personal care products involves:

**Ingredient sourcing:** 95% or more of ingredients (by weight, excluding water and salt) must be certified organic. Organic ingredients are sourced from certified organic operations.

**Prohibited substances:** Synthetic substances are restricted to those on the USDA National List. Petroleum derivatives, synthetic preservatives (parabens, phenoxyethanol), synthetic fragrances, and formaldehyde releasers are generally not permitted.

**Annual inspection:** Under the program, a USDA-accredited certifying agent inspects certified operations annually, and certifiers may conduct unannounced inspections.

**Supply chain documentation:** Certified operations keep records that let the certifier trace organic ingredients through handling and processing to the final product.

**Processing standards:** Under the program, facilities must prevent commingling with and contamination from non-organic substances. Shared equipment calls for documented cleaning protocols.

**Label compliance:** Under the program, the USDA Organic seal is reserved for products certified under the program, so the seal is the clearest signal a shopper can look for.

## Ingredient quality differences

| Ingredient | Organic grade | Conventional grade |
|-----------|--------------|-------------------|
| Shea butter | Cold-pressed from certified organic shea nuts, unrefined, full nutrient profile intact | Refined, deodorized, bleached — strips vitamins A, E, F and essential fatty acids |
| Coconut oil | Virgin cold-pressed from organic coconuts, lauric acid intact | RBD (refined, bleached, deodorized), reduced antimicrobial activity |
| Jojoba oil | Cold-pressed from organic jojoba seeds, full wax ester profile | Solvent-extracted, potentially hexane residues |
| Aloe vera | Certified organic whole-leaf or inner-fillet, no acemannan degradation | Reconstituted from powder, often 1-5% actual aloe with thickeners |
| Vitamin E | d-alpha-tocopherol from organic plant sources | dl-alpha-tocopherol (synthetic, 50% less bioactive) |
| Beeswax | Organic-certified apiaries, no pesticide exposure | Conventional hives treated with miticides, pesticide residues documented |
| Essential oils | Steam-distilled from organic botanicals | Conventional farming with pesticide residues |
| Rosemary extract | Supercritical CO2 extraction from organic rosemary | Solvent extraction, potential residues |

## Why certification matters

Without certification, "organic" claims rely entirely on brand trust. Certification provides:

**Supply chain verification:** Independent auditors review organic sourcing — not the brand's marketing department.

**Banned substance enforcement:** Under the USDA program, certifiers inspect operations and may sample products for residue testing. Conventional cosmetics are not part of this certification process.

**Accountability structure:** Under the USDA program, certificates can be suspended or revoked for non-compliance. Self-declared "natural" or "clean" claims sit outside that certification system.

**Consumer verification:** The USDA Organic Integrity Database lists certified operations, so consumers can look up a brand's USDA organic certification. For "natural" or "clean" claims, shoppers rely on the brand's own disclosures.

## Efficacy comparison

| Factor | Organic moisturizer | Conventional moisturizer |
|--------|-------------------|------------------------|
| Barrier repair | Plant lipids similar to the skin's own help support the skin barrier | Petroleum occlusion — seals the surface to reduce moisture loss |
| Nutrient delivery | Vitamins, antioxidants, and fatty acids retained through cold-pressing | Some nutrients reduced during refinement/processing |
| Anti-inflammatory activity | Whole-plant extracts retain their natural compound profiles | Isolated synthetic compounds or none |
| Long-term skin health | Supports the skin's natural moisture balance | Relies on occlusion rather than replenishing lipids |
| Sensitization risk | Often lower — fewer synthetic compounds, often fragrance-free | Can be higher — preservatives, fragrance, and surfactants are common sensitizers |
| Shelf life | 12-24 months (natural preservation) | 36-60 months (synthetic preservation) |

## Price comparison analysis

| Factor | Organic | Conventional |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| Raw ingredient cost | Often several times higher (certified organic shea butter vs refined commercial) | Petroleum derivatives cost pennies per kg |
| Certification cost | Fees charged by the USDA-accredited certifying agent, which vary by certifier | None |
| Supply chain complexity | More sourcing paperwork and supplier vetting | No organic supply chain review |
| Manufacturing constraints | USDA program calls for preventing contamination (dedicated equipment or documented cleaning) | Standard shared manufacturing |
| Shelf life (replacement frequency) | 12-24 months | 36-60 months |
| Cost per oz (typical) | $3-8/oz | $0.50-2/oz |
| Cost per effective application | Can be comparable when a formula is richer and less is used per application | Lower per application |

## The "organic premium" calculation

Surface-level price comparison favors conventional products: $15 for 16oz CeraVe vs $30 for 4oz organic moisturizer. This comparison ignores:

**Concentration:** Organic moisturizers often use concentrated plant ingredients. Conventional products often use a petroleum/water base. Less product may be needed per application.

**Multi-functionality:** Organic formulations built around nourishing botanical ingredients can simplify a routine. For the complete routine, see the [skin care guide](https://www.eraorganics.com/blogs/beauty-guide/skin-care).

**Comfort for dry, eczema-prone skin:** Regular moisturizing helps protect the skin barrier and keeps dry, eczema-prone skin comfortable, which many people find reduces how often they reach for other products. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that eczema care can carry significant ongoing costs.

**Cumulative exposure reduction:** Choosing formulas without synthetic preservatives, fragrances, and petroleum derivatives limits exposure to common sensitizers.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does "organic" mean the product works better?**
Organic certification verifies ingredient sourcing and purity — not efficacy claims. A poorly formulated organic product underperforms a well-formulated conventional one. The advantage: less-refined organic ingredients tend to retain more of their natural compounds (vitamins, antioxidants, and fatty acids) compared to refined conventional equivalents.

**Can conventional moisturizers be safe?**
Many conventional moisturizers are well tolerated. The question some shoppers raise is cumulative lifetime exposure to synthetic preservatives, fragrances, and petroleum derivatives applied daily for decades. Ingredient safety reviews typically assess ingredients one at a time, which is why some people prefer simpler formulas — for the current rules, confirm with the FDA.

**Why don't more brands get USDA Organic certification?**
Cost and complexity. Under the USDA National Organic Program, certification involves organic-grade ingredients (expensive), supply chain documentation (administrative burden), annual inspections by an accredited certifier (time and money), and contamination controls in manufacturing (operational constraints). Many brands find it cheaper to use unverified "natural" or "clean" marketing claims.

**Is "made with organic ingredients" good enough?**
Under the USDA National Organic Program, this category permits up to 30% non-organic ingredients, within the program's limits on allowed substances. The word "organic" on these labels can suggest more than the full formula delivers. Meaningful improvement over fully conventional, but not equivalent to USDA Organic.

**How do I verify a brand's organic certification?**
Search the USDA Organic Integrity Database (ams.usda.gov/integrity-database). Enter the brand or company name. Certified operations appear with their certifying agent, certificate number, and certification scope. If a brand doesn't appear there, treat any USDA Organic claim on its packaging with caution.

**Do organic moisturizers expire too quickly?**
Natural preservation systems (rosemary extract, vitamin E) typically maintain product integrity for 12-24 months. Conventional products often last 36-60 months using synthetic preservatives. The trade-off: shorter shelf life in exchange for avoiding synthetic preservatives. Purchase sizes matched to usage rate eliminate waste.
